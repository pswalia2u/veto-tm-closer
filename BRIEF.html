What is transaction monitoring?

A short introduction for anyone new to financial crime.

Criminals need banks and payment firms to move money: to hide where it came from, to pay for crime, or to pass on money stolen from scam victims. So every bank, payment firm and investment platform has to watch the money moving through its customers' accounts for signs of crime, and report what it suspects. Watching for those signs is called transaction monitoring.

Why firms must do it

  • The law requires it. In the UK, firms must monitor their customers' activity and report suspicion to the National Crime Agency. Firms that fail to can be fined heavily.
  • It stops real harm. It catches money laundering, the proceeds of fraud and scams, and money moving to fund terrorism.
  • It takes a lot of work. Automatic rules raise many alerts, and most turn out to have an ordinary explanation. Each one still needs a person to review it, which is why a system that closes the explained ones safely is valuable.

Who does what

The customer
A person or a business with an account. They move money, and they are the one asked for documents in an RFI.
The analyst, the user of your system
Works through the queue of alerts. For each one they gather the evidence and decide: close it, ask the customer, or escalate. This review is called triage, and it is the step your system does.
The MLRO
The Money Laundering Reporting Officer: the senior person every regulated firm must appoint. They review escalated alerts and decide whether to file a suspicious activity report with the authorities.

The challenge

You get

4,000 development cases, 1,000 from each institution, with each institution's SOP. Every case has its alerts, the customer's profile, their transactions, the documents on file, earlier reviews and the institution's internal list. The cases have no answers.

You build

A system that reads a folder of cases and writes a verdict, a reason and the evidence it relied on for every case. It can use rules, language models or both.

We test

Your system, five times, on 800 cases it has never seen, 200 from each institution, from the same institutions, in the same format and under the same policies. Those unseen cases decide the result.

How a case closes

Every case ends in one of three verdicts, with a reason.

CLOSE

The evidence explains all the activity the alerts raised and it fits what the institution knows about the customer. At the payments and FX firm, a held payment is released.

RFI

Request for information: nothing is suspicious yet, and one specific document or answer from the customer would settle the case. The reason must say what to ask for.

ESCALATE

Escalate to the MLRO: the activity is unexplained or there are signs of crime. The MLRO decides whether to report it.

Once there is suspicion, escalate rather than ask the customer. Asking questions at that point risks tipping them off, which is an offence.

One case gets one verdict, even when several rules fired. Close a case only when the evidence explains every alert in it. At the payments and FX firm, an outgoing payment held by an alert is one case. Everywhere else, a case is one customer over the review window.

Policy settings to apply

The SOPs set these out in full. They are the ones that most often decide a case.

Own accounts

Money moving between the customer's own accounts explains activity only when a document on file shows the customer holds the other account. When such transfers bring activity to five times the declared income or volume or more, send an RFI for an updated expected income or volume. Below five times, close.

The internal list

A match on one detail alone does not prove it is the same party. Close a list match only when the evidence shows the counterparty is someone else. If nothing settles it either way, send an RFI. If the listed party is the counterparty or controls it, escalate.

Earlier reviews and invoices

An earlier review covers only what it describes, never new or larger activity. At the payments and FX firm, a payment of £10,000 or more to anyone other than the client needs an invoice or contract on file before it is released.

Context, not triggers: round amounts, a shared device, IP address or contact details, a recipient added the same day, and crypto or gambling counterparties add context to a case. None of them raises an alert on its own.

How systems are assessed

We run your system five times on the unseen cases and look at four things.

  1. The most correct results with the fewest mistakes. Close as many closable cases as you can while making as few wrong decisions as possible. A wrongful close, closing a case that needed an RFI or an escalation, is the worst mistake. Escalating everything is penalised too. Each decision is costed per case, and lower is better:
    Right verdictYou sent CLOSEYou sent RFIYou sent ESCALATE
    CLOSE033
    RFI1002
    ESCALATE1030
  2. Explainable evidence. Every verdict needs a reason code and a reason that names the specific facts it rests on, and evidence lists the IDs of the documents, transactions or list entries relied on. Cited IDs must exist in the case's files, and the evidence that decides the case should be among them.
  3. The same results every time. The five runs must give the same verdicts and reason codes, and ideally the same evidence. A system whose answer changes from run to run cannot be trusted with an alert queue.
  4. Precision on verdict and reason. For every case with the right verdict, the reason is scored on three things: whether evidence includes the documents that decide the case, whether the reason gives the grounds the right answer relies on, and whether it names this case's own documents, people and amounts rather than wording that would fit any case. A correct verdict with a generic or wrong reason scores little.
